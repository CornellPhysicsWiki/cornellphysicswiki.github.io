# Getting Involved in Research

Undergraduate research is valuable preparation for those planning to pursue physics in graduate school, and it can also help you explore your interests and build skills for careers in industry and other fields. There are many ways to get involved, including directly emailing professors whose research interests you. Industry internships and summer research programs are also helpful ways to gain experience.

## Getting Started

Our SPS presentation, [Getting Involved in Research](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1IdmuRuRiQ7I3j2r2cILyp0RNJg2Ce8tDeIcyUPjBFAY/edit?usp=sharing), walks through finding an advisor, learning about their work, preparing a resume, and contacting professors. It includes an email template, advice on meeting with potential advisors, and an overview of what professors look for in undergraduate researchers.

You do not need to understand every detail of a group's research before reaching out. Start with topics that interest you and be ready to explain what you would like to learn.

## Cornell Resources

### Finding Groups and Professors

Areas of research at Cornell that are of interest to physics students are listed by category on the [Areas of Study](/areas_of_study.html) page. Research opportunities also extend beyond the Physics Department, including Applied and Engineering Physics, Astronomy, and other engineering departments.

- [Physics Research Opportunities](https://physics.cornell.edu/student-research-department): Project descriptions and faculty contacts for advertised undergraduate positions. Many positions are not advertised, so you can also contact professors directly.
- [Quantum Science and Engineering Researchers](https://quantum.cornell.edu/researchers/): A directory of researchers across departments. Use it to find faculty working on quantum topics and explore their group websites.
- [Engineering Research Areas](https://www.duffield.cornell.edu/core-areas-of-excellence-and-priority-research-directions/): Explore research themes across engineering to identify related fields and departments.

### Guidance and Mentorship

- [A&S Undergraduate Research](https://as.cornell.edu/research/undergraduate-research): An overview of undergraduate research in the College of Arts & Sciences.
- [Cornell Undergraduate Research: First Steps](https://undergraduateresearch.cornell.edu/getting-started/first-steps/): University guidance on getting started in research.
- [CURB Peer Mentorship Program](https://www.cornellcurb.com/pmp): Help finding and navigating research opportunities from an undergraduate mentor.
- [CURB Connect](https://www.cornellcurb.com/curb-connect): Opportunities to meet professors and learn about their research.
- [Engineering Rising Researchers Program](https://www.duffield.cornell.edu/undergraduate-research/research-opportunities/rising-researchers-program/): A structured research and mentorship program for first- and second-year Engineering students. See the program page for eligibility and participation requirements.
- [Physics Research Resources](https://physics.cornell.edu/research-resources): Additional links to research support, funding agencies, scientific literature, and tools.

## Choosing a Research Opportunity

Along with the research topic, consider the mentorship and expectations of the group. Before committing, discuss what your project would involve, who would mentor you, the expected time commitment, and whether the position offers pay or academic credit. Choose an opportunity that fits your interests, goals, and schedule.

For more information, see Cornell's guidance on [Academic Credit](https://undergraduateresearch.cornell.edu/getting-started/academic-credit/). Resources for finding research funding are listed on our [Scholarships, Funding, and Awards](/experiences/scholarships_funding.html) page.

## Making the Most of Research

Research is an opportunity to learn what kinds of problems and day-to-day work you enjoy. Ask questions, keep notes on your progress, and communicate regularly with your mentor. Keep track of your contributions and the skills you develop so you can describe your experience in future applications and interviews.

Presenting your work at group meetings or events such as the [CURB Spring Symposium](https://www.cornellcurb.com/spring-symposium) can help you practice explaining your research and receive feedback.

Publications can be a valuable outcome, but they are not the only measure of a worthwhile research experience. Learning new skills, contributing to a project, and developing relationships with mentors are valuable outcomes too.
